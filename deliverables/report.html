Do coupons create sales, or just give money away?

The question

What did the coupons buy

A supermarket mails coupons hoping customers will come in more often and buy more. But the customer may use the coupon on the milk they were going to buy anyway. In that case the store has simply given away margin.

To know whether a coupon worked, we need something that can never be observed: how much the same household would have spent had it not received the coupon. That is the problem causal inference sets out to solve.

The data is dunnhumby's public "The Complete Journey": every purchase made by households at a US supermarket chain over about two years, the coupon campaigns run in that time, who received them and who redeemed.

First impression

Households that got coupons spend times as much

The most direct approach is to compare households that received coupons with households that never did. Once campaigns are running, the first group spends about a week and the second only . Coupons look spectacularly effective.

But look at the left pair of bars. Before a single coupon was sent, the two groups were already times apart. The gap was not caused by the coupons. It was caused by who the retailer chose.

Trap one

The retailer sends coupons to the people who shop most often

The data does not say how the retailer decides who gets a coupon, but it can be worked out. Take the largest campaign (starts in week 60, 1,076 recipients). The number of weeks a household shopped in weeks 35–43 almost fully determines whether it received the campaign.

Of the households that did not shop once in those 9 weeks, none received the campaign. Of those that shopped in 7 or more of them, close to nine in ten did. The window ends weeks before the campaign starts: the retailer fixed its list about four months ahead, from the purchase records of that time.

This is bad news and good news. The bad news is that recipients and non-recipients are not comparable at all. The good news is that the selection rule can be recovered from the data, so we know what a proper comparison household looks like: one that was just as active in the same weeks but was not chosen.

Trap two

Households are chosen at a high point

A household's shopping goes up and down from month to month. The retailer picks households that were especially active in those 9 weeks, and some of them just happened to buy a lot then. Afterwards their spending drifts back down, even if they never receive a coupon.

These households spent a week during the selection window. Before receiving any coupon, their spending had already fallen by . This is regression to the mean.

If we use the households that were not chosen as the reference in a before/after comparison, recipients' spending appears to fall relative to them, and we would conclude that coupons make people spend less.

Our approach

Find households that were just as active but not chosen yet

We look only at the moment each household receives a campaign for the first time: households in all. The control group is the households that had not received any campaign by then.

Each control household then gets a weight, chosen so that the weighted control group matches the recipients on pre-campaign spending, number of trips and how often they came in. Controls that bought a lot beforehand get a large weight; those that bought little get a small one.

Finally we compare the change in the two groups from before to after the campaign. This is difference-in-differences.

The check window is the most important part of the design. Difference-in-differences assumes that without coupons the two groups would have moved in the same way. That cannot be verified directly. So we leave the last 8 weeks before the campaign unused and see whether the groups really do move together while nobody has received a coupon yet. If they do not, the later results cannot be trusted.

Why nothing shows up

Most coupons were never used

The estimate for total spending is noisy and can only rule out large effects. So look from another angle: for coupons to create new sales, somebody has to use them first.

Of the recipient households, never redeemed a single campaign coupon. Redemption is highly concentrated: households ( of recipients) account for half of all redemptions.

Coupons are used mainly by the households that already buy the most. The people who use coupons are exactly the people who least need a coupon to come in.

The coupons that were used

They bought what these households were buying already

A more sensitive test

Recipients did not shift more of their spending to promoted products

Perhaps coupons work as a reminder: households buy more of the promoted products after receiving them, without using the coupon at the till. If so, the share of recipients' spending that goes to promoted products should rise.

This measure is far more sensitive than total spending. Whether a household buys more or less in a given week moves promoted and other products alike, and taking a share cancels that swing.

In the large personalized campaigns, promoted products make up of recipients' spending before the campaign and during it. Relative to households not sent the campaign, the share changes by only percentage points, with a confidence interval whose upper end is below 1 point.

The coupons did not change what people buy.

A tempting mistake

"Customers who used a coupon spent more"

Many marketing reports compare people who used a coupon with people who did not. Done that way, households that redeemed spend a week more than the controls during the campaign.

But in the 8 weeks before the campaign started, they were already spending more. The households that go on to use a coupon are the ones whose buying was already rising. Whether to redeem is the customer's own choice after the coupon arrives; splitting on it treats an outcome as a cause.

That is why this study compares households by whether they received coupons, not by whether they used them.

Does it hold up

Different methods, samples and controls give the same answer

We re-estimated the effect during the campaign in several other ways. Each one is close to zero, and each confidence interval includes zero.

Conclusion and recommendations

The coupons mostly subsidized purchases that would have happened anyway

In this data, targeted coupon campaigns produced no detectable increase in spending. All the evidence points the same way: coupons went to the most loyal customers, most were never used, and the ones that were used went on things those customers were already buying.

01

Hold out a random control group in every campaign

Leave 5–10% of the targeted households, chosen at random, without coupons. The lift can then be measured directly, with no adjustment at all. The cost is only that a few customers get their coupons one campaign later.

02

Rethink who gets coupons

The current rule sends coupons to whoever buys most. Send some to customers whose shopping frequency is middling or falling. They are the ones a coupon can still change.

03

Rethink which coupons to send

Only a coupon for a category the customer has not bought can create a new purchase. Today just of redemptions go on a new category.

04

Judge campaigns by incremental sales, not redemption rate

A high redemption rate only says the coupons were used. It does not say they brought in new spending.

Appendix: method and limitations

Sample. The first 16 weeks of the data are a ramp-up during which households enter the record gradually. Outcomes use weeks 17–101 only. The main analysis keeps the households that already appear by week 16.

Treated and controls. The treated are five cohorts of households receiving a large campaign for the first time, households in total. The controls for each cohort are the households that have not received any campaign by week 12 after that cohort's campaign starts.

Weighting. Entropy balancing gives the controls weights so that they match the treated on mean weekly spending, mean weekly trips and the share of weeks with a visit in four pre-campaign periods (12 measures). Before weighting, the mean absolute standardized difference between the groups is ; after weighting, the largest is .

Estimator. The main estimator is doubly robust: entropy balancing followed by a regression of the change in spending on the pre-campaign measures, fitted on the controls. Used on their own, regression adjustment gives and entropy balancing .

Confidence intervals. Household-level bootstrap with 1,000 replications, redoing the weighting and regression each time.

Limitations. This is not a randomized experiment. The conclusion rests on one assumption: households with the same pre-campaign purchase records would have followed the same path afterwards without coupons. The check window supports it but cannot prove it. Comparable controls are scarce (an effective sample size of a few hundred after weighting), so the confidence interval for total spending is about ±10%. Only the 12 weeks after a first campaign are estimated. The data comes from a single retailer.
