Part 1The puzzle

The question

1What did the coupons buy?

A supermarket mails coupons hoping customers will come in more often and buy more. But the customer may use the coupon on the milk they were going to buy anyway. In that case the store has simply given away margin.

To know whether a coupon worked, we need something that can never be observed: how much the same household would have spent had it not received the coupon. That is the problem causal inference sets out to solve.

The data is dunnhumby’s public “The Complete Journey”: every purchase made by households at a US supermarket chain over about two years, the coupon campaigns run in that time, who received them and who redeemed.

Same household · without it $ ? Weeks 0–6never happened Counterfactual

The causal effect is the gap between the two receipts. We can only ever print the first one, so the second has to be estimated from comparable households.

First impression

2Households that got coupons spend times as much

The most direct approach is to compare households that received coupons with households that never did. Once campaigns are running, the first group spends about a week and the second only . Coupons look spectacularly effective. Switch to the second view to see what happens when you compare like with like.

Before a single coupon was sent, the two groups were already times apart.The gap was not caused by the coupons. It was caused by who the retailer chose.

Simpson’s paradox at work.Across everyone, recipients spend a week more. Inside the most active group, where most recipients are, the gap is . Splitting the data by hand is still too coarse; to do it properly we need to say exactly what has to be held equal.

Trap one

3The retailer sends coupons to the people who shop most often

The data does not say how the retailer decides who gets a coupon, but it can be worked out. Take the largest campaign (starts in week 60, 1,076 recipients). The number of weeks a household shopped in weeks 35–43 almost fully determines whether it received the campaign. Hover over the bars for the group sizes.

This is bad news and good news.Recipients and non-recipients are not comparable at all. But the selection rule can be recovered from the data, so we know what a proper comparison household looks like: one that was just as active in the same weeks but was not chosen.

Trap two

4Households are chosen at a high point

A household’s shopping goes up and down from month to month. The retailer picks households that were especially active in those 9 weeks, and some of them just happened to buy a lot then. Afterwards their spending drifts back down, even if they never receive a coupon.

These households spent a week during the selection window. Before receiving any coupon, their spending had already fallen by . This is regression to the mean. Each of the two simple comparisons falls into one of the traps:

Part 2The method

The causal graph

5What to adjust for, and what to leave alone

A causal graph writes down who influences whom. Each arrow is a claim: past shopping habits drive both who gets a coupon and how much a household spends; a coupon can only be redeemed if it was received; households that shop more in a given week both redeem more and spend more. Step through the patterns to see what each one means for the analysis.

We also tested a simpler graph, in which the retailer looks at just one 9-week window. The data reject it: other weeks still help predict who gets a coupon. So the analysis adjusts for the whole pre-campaign history, twelve measures over four periods.

Our approach

6Find households that were just as active but not chosen yet

We look only at the moment each household receives a campaign for the first time: households in all. The control group is the households that had not received any campaign by then. Each control household gets a weight, chosen so that the weighted control group matches the recipients on pre-campaign spending, number of trips and how often they came in. Then we compare the change in the two groups from a baseline window. Click a period on the timeline to see its job.

The check window is the most important part of the design.The comparison assumes that without coupons the two groups would have moved in the same way. That cannot be verified directly. So we leave the last 8 weeks before the campaign out of the weighting and see whether the groups really do move together while nobody has received a coupon yet.

The accurate statementNo effect was detected, and a lift larger than about can be ruled out. That is not the same as proving the effect is zero.

A second opinion

8 estimators, one test, the same answer

We repeated the estimate with the standard toolkit of causal inference: regression with covariates, four ways of using propensity scores, the meta-learners, double machine learning, a doubly robust learner and a causal forest. Each method is judged by the same placebo test: we run it on spending in the 8 weeks before the campaign, when nobody had a coupon. The true effect there is zero, so whatever a method reports is its own bias.

Part 4Why nothing shows up

Unused coupons

10Most coupons were never used

The estimate for total spending is noisy and can only rule out large effects. So look from another angle: for coupons to create new sales, somebody has to use them first.

Of the recipient households, never redeemed a single campaign coupon. Redemption is highly concentrated: households ( of recipients) account for half of all redemptions.

The coupons that were used

11They bought what these households were buying already

We matched each redemption back to that day’s receipt to find the redeemed product, and checked whether the household had bought it in the six months before the campaign started.

Compared with what the same households bought without a coupon in the same weeks, the coupon purchases are more familiar, not less. The most redeemed products are ice cream, cold cereal, frozen meat, cheese, frozen pizza and milk.

A more sensitive test

12Recipients did not shift more of their spending to promoted products

Perhaps coupons work as a reminder: households buy more of the promoted products after receiving them, without using the coupon at the till. If so, the share of recipients’ spending that goes to promoted products should rise.

This measure is far more sensitive than total spending: a busy or quiet week moves promoted and other products alike, and taking a share cancels that swing.

Relative to households not sent the campaign, the share changes by only percentage points, with a confidence interval whose upper end is below 1 point. The coupons did not change what people buy.

A tempting mistake

13“Customers who used a coupon spent more”

Many marketing reports compare people who used a coupon with people who did not. Done that way, the first-time recipients who redeemed seem to respond strongly. Now look at the same comparison before any coupon arrived.

During the campaign
a week more than weighted controls
In the 8 weeks before any coupon arrived
a week more, with no coupon to explain it

The households that go on to use a coupon are the ones whose buying was already rising. Whether to redeem is the customer’s own choice after the coupon arrives; splitting on it treats an outcome as a cause.

Redemption is a colliderIt is caused by the coupon and by how much the household happens to shop. Splitting on it lets that shopping leak into the comparison, so this study compares households by whether they received coupons, not whether they used them.

Who responds

14No type of household shows a clear response

Perhaps the average hides a group that does respond. A causal forest estimates an effect for every recipient household; here those are averaged by how often the household shopped before the campaign.

The estimates rise slightly with shopping frequency, but every interval includes zero and they all overlap. There is no group for which the campaign visibly pays off.

Conclusion and recommendations

16The coupons mostly subsidized purchases that would have happened anyway

In this data, targeted coupon campaigns produced no detectable increase in spending. All the evidence points the same way: coupons went to the most loyal customers, most were never used, and the ones that were used went on things those customers were already buying.

Measure

Hold out a random control group in every campaign

Leave 5–10% of the targeted households without coupons, chosen at random. The lift can then be measured directly, with no adjustment at all.

Target

Rethink who gets coupons

The current rule sends coupons to whoever buys most. Send some to customers whose shopping is middling or falling. They are the ones a coupon can still change.

Offer

Rethink which coupons to send

Only a coupon for a category the customer has not bought can create a new purchase. Today just of redemptions go on a new category.

Judge

Count incremental sales, not redemptions

A high redemption rate only says the coupons were used. It does not say they brought in new spending.

Appendix

AMethod and limitations

Sample

The first 16 weeks of the data are a ramp-up during which households enter the record gradually. Outcomes use weeks 17–101 only. The main analysis keeps the households that already appear by week 16.

Treated and controls

The treated are five cohorts of households receiving a large campaign for the first time, households in total. The controls for each cohort are the households that have not received any campaign by week 12 after that cohort’s campaign starts.

Weighting

Entropy balancing gives the controls weights so that they match the treated on mean weekly spending, mean weekly trips and the share of weeks with a visit in four pre-campaign periods (12 measures). Before weighting, the mean absolute standardized difference between the groups is ; after weighting, the largest is .

Estimator

The main estimator is doubly robust: entropy balancing followed by a regression of the change in spending on the pre-campaign measures, fitted on the controls. Used on their own, regression adjustment gives and entropy balancing .

Confidence intervals

Household-level bootstrap with 1,000 replications, redoing the weighting and regression each time.

Limitations

This is not a randomized experiment. The conclusion rests on one assumption: households with the same pre-campaign purchase records would have followed the same path afterwards without coupons. The check window supports it but cannot prove it. Comparable controls are scarce (an effective sample size of a few hundred after weighting), so the confidence interval for total spending is about ±10%. Only the 12 weeks after a first campaign are estimated, and the data comes from a single retailer.

Other checks

Dividing the campaign’s effect by the of first-time recipients who redeem gives the effect per redeeming household (an instrumental-variable reading): a week, with an interval from to , too wide to say anything. About of recipients’ store visits during a campaign can be attributed to it, at most . A Bayesian network learned from the data keeps arrows from shopping habits to the coupon and to spending, and none from the coupon to spending.

A caveat on the check window

Spending in the 8 weeks just before a campaign still helps predict who receives it, even after the twelve measures. The leftover bias is small and pushes estimates up, so it cannot be hiding a positive effect; adjusting on every week before the campaign gives .
